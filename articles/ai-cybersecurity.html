AI and Cybersecurity: How Digital Defence Is Evolving

By Global Insight Editorial Desk

Technology Explainer · 7-minute read

Artificial intelligence is changing how organisations detect suspicious activity, investigate security alerts and protect digital systems. At the same time, criminals can misuse AI to make some scams more convincing and increase the scale of certain attacks.

This creates a changing cybersecurity environment. AI can strengthen digital defence, but it is not a replacement for secure systems, careful decisions and well-trained people.

What Is AI-Powered Cybersecurity?

AI-powered cybersecurity uses machine-learning models and related techniques to help identify unusual behaviour, analyse large amounts of security data and prioritise potential threats.

For example, a security system may flag a login that differs from a user's usual pattern. Analysts can then investigate whether the activity is legitimate or requires further action.

These systems can make mistakes. Unusual activity may be harmless, while a real threat may resemble normal behaviour. Human review and appropriate testing remain important.

How AI Can Help Security Teams

  • Threat detection: Analysing system activity to identify suspicious patterns.
  • Alert prioritisation: Helping teams focus on incidents that may require urgent attention.
  • Incident investigation: Summarising logs and organising information for analysts.
  • Fraud monitoring: Highlighting transactions or account behaviour that may deserve review.
  • Security awareness: Helping organisations develop training and explain common scam patterns.

How Criminals Can Misuse AI

AI can help criminals create more fluent scam messages, translate fraudulent communications and imitate the style of legitimate organisations. Voice and image manipulation can also be misused to impersonate other people.

These capabilities make it more important to verify unusual requests, especially messages asking for money, passwords, verification codes or confidential information.

However, not every suspicious message uses AI, and AI-generated content is not always malicious. Security decisions should be based on context and verification rather than assumptions about how a message looks.

Why Phishing Remains a Serious Risk

Phishing attempts try to trick people into revealing information, opening unsafe attachments or visiting fraudulent websites. A message may appear to come from a bank, employer, delivery company or someone the recipient knows.

Good spelling and professional-looking graphics are not proof that a message is genuine. If a request is unexpected, use a known official website or telephone number to confirm it independently.

Practical Ways to Protect Your Accounts

  • Use a different, strong password for each important account.
  • Enable multifactor authentication wherever it is available.
  • Keep your operating system, browser and applications updated.
  • Never share one-time verification codes or passwords in response to unsolicited messages.
  • Verify urgent payment requests through a separate, trusted communication channel.
  • Keep important files backed up and protect access to backup accounts.
  • Review account activity and report suspicious access promptly.

Challenges for Businesses

Organisations must consider how AI tools handle confidential data, whether their systems produce reliable alerts and who is responsible when automated decisions go wrong.

A useful security programme combines technical controls, employee training, access management, incident response plans and regular testing. AI should be introduced with clear boundaries and a way to review its decisions.

What About AI Privacy?

Before using an AI service for work, users should understand its data policies and their organisation's rules. Confidential documents, customer records, passwords and other sensitive information should not be entered into tools that are not approved for that purpose.

Businesses should also assess how AI systems store information, who can access it and whether their use complies with applicable privacy and security requirements.

The Future of Cybersecurity

AI is likely to become a more common part of security operations, but effective protection will continue to depend on sound engineering, timely updates, reliable processes and informed people.

The strongest approach is not to trust automation blindly. It is to use AI where it provides measurable value, test its limitations and ensure that important security decisions receive appropriate oversight.

Conclusion

AI is creating new opportunities for detecting threats while also introducing new challenges around scams, privacy and automated decision-making. Individuals can reduce many common risks by protecting their accounts and independently verifying unusual requests.

For businesses, a layered security strategy remains essential: AI can support the work, but responsible policies and reliable security practices provide the foundation.

Practical steps for using AI tools more safely

AI can help security teams sort alerts and investigate patterns, but attackers can also use automation to scale phishing, impersonation and reconnaissance. Organisations should not assume that adding an AI feature automatically improves security.

  • Protect accounts: use multifactor authentication where available, unique passwords and least-privilege access.
  • Verify unusual requests: confirm payment changes, password resets and urgent requests through a separate trusted channel.
  • Keep systems updated: patch operating systems, browsers, applications and important dependencies.
  • Limit data exposure: do not paste secrets, credentials or confidential customer information into tools without approved protections.
  • Review automated actions: require human approval for consequential changes and retain logs that help investigate incidents.
  • Practise response: make sure staff know how to report suspicious activity and what to do after a suspected compromise.

These basics complement rather than replace a risk-based security programme. The CISA Secure Our World guidance offers accessible steps for individuals and organisations, while the NIST AI Risk Management Framework can help teams think systematically about risks from AI systems.
