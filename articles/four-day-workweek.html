The Four-Day Workweek: Can Companies Work Less and Achieve More?

Workplace Explainer · 6-minute read

The traditional five-day working week is being reconsidered by some employers as they look for ways to improve employee well-being, retain talent and organise work more efficiently. One alternative receiving attention is the four-day workweek.

The idea is straightforward: employees work across four days instead of five. However, the exact arrangement varies. Some employers reduce weekly hours without reducing pay, while others compress the same hours into fewer days.

What Is a Four-Day Workweek?

A four-day workweek generally means scheduling work over four days rather than five. The most discussed version reduces working hours while keeping employees' pay unchanged.

A compressed schedule is different. For example, an employee might work four longer days instead of five shorter ones. This reduces the number of commuting days but does not necessarily reduce total hours.

Understanding the difference matters because fewer working days do not automatically mean less work or more free time.

Why Are Employers Testing It?

Employers exploring shorter working weeks often want to improve recruitment, reduce burnout, increase job satisfaction and encourage employees to focus on high-value tasks.

Supporters argue that a shorter schedule can encourage teams to reduce unnecessary meetings, clarify responsibilities and organise work more effectively. The results, however, depend on how the change is planned and implemented.

What Have Trials Found?

A notable UK four-day-week pilot conducted in 2022 involved dozens of participating organisations. The trial reported encouraging results for many participating businesses and workers, including improvements in well-being and continued participation by many companies after the pilot.

These findings are promising, but they should be interpreted carefully. Participating employers were not necessarily representative of every industry, and results from a voluntary trial may not apply equally to every workplace.

A shorter workweek is not a guaranteed productivity boost. Businesses need to evaluate service quality, workload, customer needs and employee outcomes over time.

Potential Benefits for Employees

  • More personal time: An additional day away from work can provide time for family, rest, education or personal responsibilities.
  • Better recovery: Some employees may find it easier to balance demanding work with their personal lives.
  • Less commuting: Fewer journeys may save time and transport costs for workers who commute in person.
  • Greater focus: Clear priorities and fewer unnecessary meetings may help employees use their working hours more effectively.

Challenges Businesses Must Consider

Not every organisation can simply close for an extra day. Hospitals, retail businesses, customer-support teams and other services may need coverage throughout the week.

If the same workload is squeezed into fewer hours without proper planning, employees may experience more pressure rather than less. Some businesses may also need additional staff or staggered schedules to maintain service.

Fairness is another issue. Employers must consider how shorter schedules affect part-time staff, hourly workers and teams whose duties cannot easily be completed remotely.

How Companies Can Test the Idea

  • Set clear goals before changing the schedule.
  • Review meetings and repetitive tasks that can be reduced.
  • Measure output and service quality rather than hours alone.
  • Ask employees about workload, stress and customer needs.
  • Run a time-limited pilot and compare results with the previous schedule.

Will It Become the New Standard?

The four-day workweek remains one of several approaches to workplace flexibility, not a universal corporate standard. Adoption will depend on industry requirements, labour markets, customer expectations and evidence from individual organisations.

Some companies may find that a shorter week works well, while others may prefer flexible start times, hybrid working or other arrangements. The most effective model is the one that supports employees while meeting real operational needs.

Conclusion

A four-day workweek offers a possible way to rethink how work is organised. Trials have produced encouraging findings, but success depends on realistic workloads, clear goals and careful measurement.

Rather than assuming fewer days always lead to better results, employers can use evidence and small-scale trials to discover which schedule works best for their people and their business.

Four-day week: two different models

The phrase “four-day workweek” can describe different arrangements. Under a reduced-hours model, employees work fewer total hours while pay is maintained. Under a compressed-hours model, the same weekly hours are fitted into four longer days. These approaches can have different effects on fatigue, care responsibilities, service coverage and productivity.

  • Define the goal: decide whether the aim is wellbeing, retention, output, recruitment or another measurable outcome.
  • Map essential coverage: plan customer support, handovers and urgent responsibilities across the week.
  • Remove low-value work: review meetings, duplicate reporting and unnecessary approvals before simply intensifying the remaining days.
  • Measure outcomes: compare output quality, overtime, absence, retention and customer experience with a baseline.
  • Check who benefits: results may differ by role, sector, caregiving needs and whether staff have real control over their schedules.

Pilot results should be read in context: participating organisations may be self-selected, and results from one group do not automatically predict what will happen elsewhere. Employers should publish the trial's design, measures and limitations rather than treating a headline percentage as universal proof.
