Pakistan’s Smog Crisis: Clean Air Compact Adopted as Pollution Remains High

A national agreement calls for coordinated action against air pollution as Lahore and other Punjab cities face unhealthy conditions ahead of the winter smog season.

By Global Insight Editorial Desk · Published October 30, 2026 · Updated October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Pakistan’s preparations for the winter smog season have gained urgency after a national conference adopted the Islamabad Clean Air Compact, while fresh monitoring figures showed very high pollution levels in several Punjab cities. The agreement sets out a framework for federal and provincial coordination, but its impact will depend on whether policy commitments are translated into funded, measurable action.

What happened at the clean-air conference?

The two-day Pakistan National Clean Air Conference concluded in Islamabad on October 29, adopting the Islamabad Clean Air Compact. According to reporting by Dawn published on October 30, participants called for coordinated federal and provincial measures, stricter enforcement, regular emissions inventories and annual reviews.

The discussions covered several major sources of pollution: transport, industrial activity, agricultural residue burning, household cooking and waste. Participants also highlighted the need for reliable monitoring networks and clear responsibilities between institutions. The compact is a policy framework; its adoption alone does not mean that air quality has already improved.

Lahore’s latest reported air-quality reading

In a separate report dated October 30, Dawn said Lahore recorded an Air Quality Index (AQI) of 419 in IQAir’s global city ranking during the 10am–11am window on October 29. The same report placed Karachi at 165 during that comparison. These are time-specific readings, not permanent city-wide conditions, and AQI can change with weather, location and monitoring method.

The Punjab government’s monitoring dashboard showed elevated readings in a number of other locations as well. The newspaper noted that the two monitoring platforms produced different figures because their monitoring locations and reporting periods were not identical. Readers should therefore check the timestamp and source whenever comparing AQI numbers.

What authorities say they are doing

The October 30 report described enforcement actions targeting smoke-emitting vehicles, brick kilns, industrial units, plastic burning and construction dust. It reported that Punjab’s Environment Protection Agency had inspected thousands of sites during a month-long campaign and taken actions against units found violating environmental rules.

At the national conference, officials and development partners also discussed practical interventions. These included better vehicle inspections, improved public transport, more efficient freight movement, cleaner industrial processes, modernised brick kilns, alternatives to crop-residue burning and access to cleaner household cooking options.

Why implementation matters more than announcements

Air pollution does not come from one source, so no single measure is likely to solve the problem. Transport emissions, industrial fuel use, dust, waste burning and agricultural practices can all contribute. The right mix of action will differ by location and season, which makes consistent monitoring and transparent public data important.

The federal climate minister also announced a planned $10 million fund for clean-air startups at the conference, according to an October 28 government press release. The announcement is a proposal whose practical effect will depend on approval, funding arrangements, selection criteria and the projects eventually supported.

Pakistan’s National Clean Air Policy provides a wider policy context and identifies transport, industry, agriculture and household sources among the areas requiring attention. The current challenge is to connect national policy with local enforcement, sustained financing and publicly reported progress.

What residents can take from the latest developments

For residents, the most useful information is current, local air-quality data and public-health guidance from relevant authorities. AQI values should be read with their reporting time and monitoring source. People who are especially sensitive to air pollution can follow advice from local health authorities and take sensible precautions when official conditions are poor.

What to watch next: Whether the Clean Air Compact leads to published emissions data, clearly assigned federal and provincial responsibilities, stable funding and regular public reviews. Those are practical tests of progress—not simply the announcement of a new agreement.

This is a developing environmental issue. Future assessments should compare readings from consistent monitoring locations and time periods, and distinguish announced plans from measures that have actually been implemented.
