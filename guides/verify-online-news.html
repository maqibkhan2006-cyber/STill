A Practical Checklist for Verifying Online News Before Sharing It

A useful news habit is to pause between seeing a claim and sharing it. This guide gives readers a repeatable process for checking where a story came from, whether its evidence supports the headline, and what context may be missing.

By Global Insight Editorial Desk · Published 30 October 2026 · Practical reference guide
How to use this guide: Work through the sections in order, use the checklist near the end, and follow the linked primary resources when you need more detail.

1. Start with the original source

Look for the earliest available source: an official statement, public document, research paper, court filing, company announcement or direct interview. A screenshot of a post is not the same as the original post. If a report links to a document, open that document and check whether it actually says what the report claims. Note who published it, when it was published and whether the author is identified.

2. Separate the claim from the commentary

Write down the exact factual claim in one sentence. Then separate it from opinion, prediction, emotional language and speculation around it. For example, “a company announced a new product” is different from “the product will transform the industry.” The first can be checked against an announcement; the second is an interpretation that needs evidence and should be presented as opinion.

3. Check dates, locations and updates

Old photographs and articles can circulate as if they describe a new event. Check the publication date, the date the event occurred and the location. Look for later corrections or updates. Search a distinctive sentence or the name of the event with a date to find earlier coverage. A recently reposted story is not necessarily recent news.

4. Corroborate independently

Find at least two credible sources that are independent of one another. Several websites repeating the same wire report or press release are not necessarily independent confirmation. Prefer sources with direct access to the evidence, transparent reporting methods and a record of corrections. For important claims, check primary records rather than relying only on summaries.

5. Inspect images and video carefully

Ask whether the visual is original, current and connected to the stated place. Search for distinctive image details or use a reverse-image search service to find earlier appearances. Check signs, weather, landmarks, language and shadows only as clues—not proof. Cropped clips can remove the sentence before or after a statement, changing its meaning. Do not treat an image match alone as confirmation.

6. Read beyond the headline

Headlines compress complicated stories. Read the full article and check whether the body supports the headline. Notice words such as “may”, “could”, “early”, “preliminary” and “according to”. They communicate uncertainty or attribution. If the evidence is a small survey, an early study or an unnamed source, the story should not be presented as settled fact.

7. Use a simple confidence note

Before sharing, record: what is confirmed; what remains uncertain; the strongest primary source; and the date you checked. If a key detail cannot be verified, say so or wait. A responsible correction should be visible and specific rather than quietly replacing the original claim.

8. A 60-second pre-share checklist

1. Can I identify the original source? 2. Is the date and location correct? 3. Does the evidence support the exact claim? 4. Have independent sources confirmed it? 5. Is the image or clip in its original context? 6. Have later updates changed the story? If several answers are no, do not present the claim as verified.

9. Limits and responsible use

This checklist reduces avoidable mistakes; it cannot guarantee that a claim is true. Breaking news may remain uncertain while events develop. Avoid naming private individuals based on unverified posts, and do not amplify sensitive personal information. When reporting on emergencies, rely on relevant public authorities for immediate instructions.
